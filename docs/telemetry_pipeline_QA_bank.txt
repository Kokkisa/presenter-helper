SECTION 1 — PROJECT OVERVIEW & MOTIVATION
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. Can you walk me through this project?

A: This is a real-time streaming pipeline for LPG pipeline telemetry.
   Coriolis mass flow meters installed at pipeline segments — Kondapalli
   inlet, Rajahmundry transfer line, Mangalore compressor discharge —
   continuously stream four parameters: mass flow rate, temperature,
   pressure, and fluid density.
   
   The pipeline does three things: first it cleans the stream — rejects
   malformed packets and deduplicates Kafka redeliveries. Then it runs
   anomaly detection on every individual reading using a rolling Z-score.
   Finally it aggregates into 1-minute windows and runs a second anomaly
   check on the window summaries to catch sustained drift.
   
   I built it because the WebCreek assessment asked for exactly this —
   streaming telemetry deduplication, aggregation, and anomaly detection.
   And it mirrors what I actually built at HPCL at production scale.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2 — ANOMALY DETECTION (ALGORITHM DEEP DIVE)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q4. What anomaly detection algorithm did you use and why?

A: Rolling Z-score with two detectors — per-record and post-window.
   
   Z = (observed_value − rolling_mean) / rolling_std
   Flag if |Z| > 3.0
   
   I chose Z-score for four reasons:
   One — no training data required. Works from the first 10 readings,
   unlike Isolation Forest which needs historical batch training.
   Two — truly streaming. Computes in O(1) per record using Welford's
   online algorithm for numerically stable incremental mean and variance.
   Three — human interpretable. I can tell an engineer "this reading
   was 4.5 sigma from normal" and they immediately understand severity.
   Four — stateless per sensor. Each sensor maintains its own rolling
   window independently, scales to thousands of sensors linearly.

---

Q5. Why not Isolation Forest? You mentioned you used it at HPCL.

A: Isolation Forest is excellent and it's what I used at HPCL — but it
   requires batch training on historical data first. You need to fit the
   forest before you can score new points. In a fresh streaming pipeline
   with no prior history, you can't use it on day one.
   
   Z-score warms up in 10 readings. In production I'd layer Isolation
   Forest on top once we have 30 days of baseline data — use Z-score for
   immediate alerting from cold start, retrain an Isolation Forest model
   weekly on the accumulated history, then run both in parallel.

---

Q6. What is Welford's algorithm and why did you use it?

A: Welford's is an online algorithm for computing mean and variance
   incrementally — one data point at a time — without accumulating the
   full history in memory.
   
   The naive approach — sum all values, divide by count — suffers from
   catastrophic cancellation when values are large and variance is small.
   For example pressure readings around 11.0 bar with variance of 0.01
   — the subtraction loses precision in floating point arithmetic.
   
   Welford's updates mean and variance in a single pass, numerically
   stable, O(1) per update, O(1) memory regardless of history length.
   For industrial sensors streaming at high frequency, this matters.

---

Q7. What's the difference between your two anomaly detectors?

A: They catch different failure modes.
   
   The per-record Z-score catches sudden spikes — a pressure reading
   that instantly jumps from 11 bar to 45 bar. It fires within one
   second of the anomalous reading arriving.
   
   The window spike detector catches sustained drift — a sensor running
   2 bar high for 30 consecutive minutes. Each individual reading might
   be within 3 sigma of its local baseline, but compared to the
   30-window historical norm the sustained elevation is clearly anomalous.
   
   In practice on LPG pipelines, sudden spikes often indicate mechanical
   events — valve slam, pump cavitation, pressure surge. Sustained drift
   often indicates calibration drift, slow leak, or gradual blockage.
   Two different engineering problems, two different detectors.

---

Q8. What other algorithms could you use for this problem?

A: Several depending on the constraints:
   
   EWMA — Exponentially Weighted Moving Average. Adapts faster to slow
   drift than Z-score because recent readings get higher weight.
   Strong alternative for industrial sensors where baseline shifts
   gradually over hours or days. Would be my first production upgrade.
   
   Isolation Forest — ensemble method, excellent at catching multivariate
   anomalies (pressure AND temperature both unusual simultaneously).
   Needs batch training but very powerful once warmed up.
   
   IQR — Interquartile Range. Simpler than Z-score, no normality
   assumption, robust to outliers in the baseline itself. Good for
   sensors with non-Gaussian distributions.
   
   LSTM Autoencoder — learns deep temporal patterns, excellent for
   seasonal or cyclical data. High operational overhead — needs GPU,
   training pipeline, model versioning, retraining schedule. Overkill
   for this use case but justified for complex multivariate anomalies.
   
   STL decomposition — Seasonal Trend decomposition. Good when sensor
   data has known periodicity — daily temperature cycles for example.

---

Q9. How do you handle the baseline warm-up period?

A: The detector has a minimum baseline samples threshold — set to 10 by
   default in config.py. Until 10 readings have been collected for a
   given sensor and metric pair, no alerts are fired regardless of how
   extreme the value looks.
   
   This prevents false positives on startup when the rolling window is
   too small to compute a meaningful standard deviation. The cost is a
   10-reading blind period per sensor — acceptable because the pipeline
   starts fresh and those first readings are establishing the baseline.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3 — DEDUPLICATION
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q10. How does your deduplication work?

A: TTL-bounded seen-set using two data structures in combination.
   A Python dict for O(1) average-case lookup by message_id, and a
   deque for O(1) eviction of expired entries.
   
   When a record arrives, I check the dict — if the message_id is
   already there, it's a duplicate, drop it. If not, I insert it into
   both the dict and the deque with the current timestamp.
   
   Every time a new record arrives I also walk the front of the deque
   and pop any entries older than the TTL window (5 minutes by default).
   This keeps memory bounded without a background cleanup thread.
   
   The cache is also hard-capped at 100,000 entries — if we hit that
   limit, the oldest entry is evicted regardless of TTL.

---

Q11. Why a deque and dict together instead of just a dict?

A: The dict alone gives fast lookup but no efficient way to find and
   remove expired entries. You'd have to scan all keys on every insert —
   O(n) per record.
   
   The deque maintains insertion order — oldest entries are always at
   the front. So eviction is O(k) where k is only the number of entries
   that actually expired, not the full cache size. In practice k is
   very small relative to cache size.

---

Q12. What's the alternative for very high cardinality — millions of sensors?

A: Bloom filter. Fixed memory regardless of cardinality, O(1) lookup,
   O(1) insert. The trade-off is a configurable false positive rate —
   around 0.1% with typical settings, meaning 1 in 1000 legitimate
   records gets incorrectly flagged as a duplicate and dropped.
   
   For telemetry pipelines where occasional missed records are less
   costly than OOM crashes, that's an acceptable trade-off. The code has
   this documented as a comment in deduplicator.py with the pybloom-live
   implementation pattern ready to swap in.

---

Q13. Why manual Kafka offset commit instead of auto-commit?

A: Auto-commit advances the offset on a timer regardless of whether
   the message was successfully processed. If the pipeline crashes
   between the auto-commit and the actual processing, that record is
   silently lost — at-most-once semantics.
   
   Manual commit means I commit the offset only after the record has
   been deduplicated, processed, and added to the aggregation window.
   Combined with the deduplicator, this gives effectively exactly-once
   semantics — Kafka provides at-least-once delivery, the deduplicator
   handles the resulting duplicates idempotently.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4 — AGGREGATION
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q14. What type of windowing did you implement and why?

A: Tumbling windows — non-overlapping, fixed-size 60-second buckets.
   Each record belongs to exactly one window. At window close I compute
   mean, min, max, standard deviation, and p95.
   
   I chose tumbling over sliding because the use case is operational
   monitoring — you want a clean "what happened in the last minute"
   summary, not overlapping summaries that double-count records.
   Sliding windows are better for things like fraud detection where you
   want continuous rolling risk scores.

---

Q15. What's the boundary bug you avoided in the aggregator?

A: A common mistake in tumbling window implementations is to call
   flush(), collect all records currently in the buffer, then prune old
   records. The bug is that pruning happens based on age from now —
   which can include records that belong to the next window.
   
   The correct approach is to first determine the window boundary
   (window_start + window_seconds), collect only records with timestamps
   at or before that boundary, prune exactly those records from the
   buffer, and start the next window at the boundary — not at now().
   
   If you start the next window at now() instead of the boundary, you
   accumulate drift. After 60 windows the window start has shifted by
   seconds and your summaries no longer align with clean minute marks.

---

Q16. Why track p95 in addition to mean and max?

A: Mean hides the tail. In a 1-minute window with 400 readings, if 20
   readings spike to 50 bar while the rest are at 11 bar, the mean
   stays around 12.4 — looks fine. Max catches the spike but doesn't
   tell you how widespread it was.
   
   P95 tells you "95% of readings were at or below this value" — so
   if p95 is 14 bar when normal max is 12, you know 5% of readings
   were elevated. That's 20 readings in a minute — not a one-off spike,
   it's a pattern. For SLA monitoring and capacity planning, p95 and p99
   are the standard metrics.

-----------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5 — ARCHITECTURE & PRODUCTION READINESS
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q17. How would you scale this to handle millions of records per second?

A: Several layers:
   
   First — Kafka partitioning. Partition by sensor_id so all readings
   from one sensor always go to the same consumer. This keeps the
   deduplication and aggregation state local to one partition consumer
   — no cross-node coordination needed.
   
   Second — horizontal scaling. Run N consumer instances, each handling
   M partitions. The deduplicator and aggregator state is per-partition,
   so no shared state between instances.
   
   Third — replace the in-process aggregator with a proper streaming
   framework. Kafka Streams or Flink both have native windowing operators
   with state stores that survive consumer restarts. The current in-memory
   aggregator loses window state on crash.
   
   Fourth — for the deduplicator at very high cardinality, move from
   the in-process dict to Redis with TTL keys. Then multiple consumer
   instances share dedup state correctly.

---

Q18. What would you change to make this truly production ready?

A: Five things:
   
   One — replace in-memory aggregator state with Kafka Streams or Flink
   state store. Current implementation loses window data on crash.
   
   Two — add schema validation via Avro or Protobuf with a Schema
   Registry. Currently we validate fields manually in parse_message().
   
   Three — add proper observability. Prometheus metrics for throughput,
   lag, alert rate, dedup rate. The pipeline already logs stats every
   30 seconds but that's not queryable.
   
   Four — replace print-based alert sink with a real integration —
   PagerDuty for critical alerts, InfluxDB or TimescaleDB for all
   records and windows, so dashboards can query the data.
   
   Five — EWMA-based adaptive thresholds. Current Z-score uses a fixed
   3-sigma threshold. A sensor that gradually drifts over weeks would
   have its baseline drift with it and never alert. EWMA with a slower
   decay rate would catch this.

---

Q19. How does your pipeline handle a sensor going offline?

A: Currently it doesn't — that's a gap I'd flag as a production
   improvement. If a sensor stops sending, the pipeline simply stops
   receiving records for that sensor_id. No alert is raised.
   
   The fix is a heartbeat monitor — a separate thread that tracks the
   last-seen timestamp per sensor and fires a MISSING_DATA alert if
   any sensor hasn't reported within N seconds. That alert type is
   already defined in the AnomalyType enum in models.py — it just
   needs the heartbeat thread wired in.

---

Q20. What does your test suite cover?

A: 23 tests across four classes.
   
   TestParsing — 6 tests covering valid records, invalid JSON, missing
   fields, non-numeric values, null values, and unix epoch timestamps.
   
   TestDeduplicator — 6 tests covering first occurrence acceptance,
   duplicate rejection, TTL expiry (same ID accepted again after TTL),
   capacity eviction at max_cache_size, and stats accuracy.
   
   TestAggregator — 5 tests covering window timing (no flush before
   window closes), correct mean/min/max computation, multi-sensor
   isolation, minimum samples guard, and the stats helper functions.
   
   TestAnomalyDetector — 6 tests covering baseline warm-up suppression,
   normal values producing no alert, spike triggering alert, alert
   containing correct baseline stats, window spike detector, and the
   composite detector combining both.

Q24. The code has no persistence — windows and alerts just print. Isn't that incomplete?

A: Intentionally. The pipeline.py has callback hooks — on_alert and
   on_window — that are currently wired to print statements. These are
   meant to be replaced with real sinks: InfluxDB writer, TimescaleDB
   insert, PagerDuty webhook, Kafka topic producer.
   
   I separated the sink from the logic deliberately — the pipeline
   doesn't care where alerts go. That's a dependency inversion pattern.
   Adding a real database sink is a one-function change without touching
   any of the core logic.

---

Q25. Why Python and not Java or Go for a high-performance streaming pipeline?

A: For this use case — demonstrating the architecture and logic — Python
   is the right choice. The assessment asked for Python specifically.
   
   In production, if throughput exceeded what a Python consumer could
   handle — roughly 50,000 to 100,000 records per second per process —
   I'd move the hot path to Java or Go. The deduplication and aggregation
   logic translates directly to Kafka Streams (Java) or a Go consumer
   with Redis state.
   
   But at the throughput levels of most industrial telemetry pipelines —
   even large ones — Python with asyncio or multiprocessing is entirely
   sufficient. Our HPCL network with 55 plants and multiple sensors per
   plant would peak at maybe 5,000 records per second. Python handles
   that comfortably.
